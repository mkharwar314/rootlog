> Understand how attackers manipulate human behaviour to obtain sensitive information, gain unauthorized access, or influence victims into performing unsafe actions.

## 1. What Is Social Engineering?

**Social Engineering** is a psychological manipulation technique used by attackers to trick people into revealing confidential information or performing actions that compromise security.

Instead of directly attacking a computer system, social engineering targets the human element of security.

Attackers exploit:

- Trust
    
- Fear
    
- Curiosity
    
- Greed
    
- Urgency
    
- Authority
    
- Lack of awareness
    

**Example:** An attacker impersonates an IT support employee and asks someone for their account password.

## 2. How Social Engineering Works

A typical social engineering attack follows these stages:

1. **Information gathering:** The attacker collects information about the target.
    
2. **Building trust:** The attacker creates a believable identity or situation.
    
3. **Manipulation:** The attacker uses psychological pressure to influence the victim.
    
4. **Exploitation:** The victim shares information, transfers money, or performs an unsafe action.
    
5. **Execution:** The attacker uses the obtained access or information.
    
6. **Concealment:** The attacker may attempt to hide their activities.
    

Not every attack follows exactly the same sequence.

## 3. Common Social Engineering Techniques

### 3.1 Phishing

Phishing uses deceptive emails, messages, or websites to steal information or deliver malware.

**Example:** A fake bank email asks the victim to verify account credentials.

See: [[Phishing]]

### 3.2 Spear Phishing

Spear phishing targets a specific person or organization using personalized information.

**Example:** An attacker sends an employee a message referring to their actual department and manager.

### 3.3 Pretexting

Pretexting involves creating a fabricated situation or identity to obtain information or access.

**Example:** Someone pretends to be an auditor and requests confidential company records.

### 3.4 Baiting

Baiting uses something attractive to encourage a victim to perform an unsafe action.

**Example:** An attacker leaves a USB drive labelled "Confidential Salary Data" in a public area, hoping someone will connect it to a computer.

### 3.5 Quid Pro Quo

Quid pro quo means offering something in exchange for information or an action.

**Example:** An attacker pretends to provide technical support in exchange for login credentials.

### 3.6 Tailgating

Tailgating occurs when an unauthorized person follows an authorized person into a restricted physical area without proper authentication.

**Example:** Someone follows an employee through a secure office entrance.

### 3.7 Shoulder Surfing

Shoulder surfing involves observing someone entering sensitive information.

**Example:** An attacker watches a person enter their ATM PIN.

### 3.8 Dumpster Diving

Dumpster diving involves searching discarded materials for sensitive information.

Examples include:

- Printed documents.
    
- Old storage devices.
    
- Employee records.
    
- Password notes.
    
- Network diagrams.
    

Organizations should securely dispose of sensitive materials.

### 3.9 Vishing

Vishing is voice-based phishing carried out through phone calls or other voice communication.

**Example:** Someone impersonates a bank employee and requests an OTP.

### 3.10 Smishing

Smishing is phishing conducted through SMS or text messages.

**Example:** A fake delivery message contains a suspicious tracking link.

## 4. Social Engineering Techniques Comparison

|Technique|Main Method|
|---|---|
|Phishing|Deceptive electronic messages|
|Spear phishing|Personalized targeted messages|
|Pretexting|Fabricated identity or situation|
|Baiting|Attractive offer or object|
|Quid pro quo|Service or benefit in exchange|
|Tailgating|Unauthorized physical entry|
|Shoulder surfing|Observing sensitive information|
|Dumpster diving|Searching discarded materials|
|Vishing|Voice-based manipulation|
|Smishing|SMS-based manipulation|

## 5. Psychological Principles Used by Attackers

Attackers frequently exploit predictable human behaviour.

### 5.1 Authority

People may follow instructions from someone who appears to be powerful or important.

**Example:** An attacker impersonates a company director.

### 5.2 Urgency

Attackers create time pressure to prevent careful thinking.

**Example:** "Your account will be disabled in five minutes."

### 5.3 Fear

Attackers threaten negative consequences.

**Example:** "Your system is infected. Pay immediately."

### 5.4 Trust

Attackers pretend to be familiar or reliable people.

**Example:** Someone impersonates a colleague.

### 5.5 Curiosity

Attackers use interesting or surprising information to attract attention.

**Example:** A message claims to contain confidential photographs.

### 5.6 Greed

Attackers offer unrealistic rewards or financial benefits.

**Example:** A fake lottery notification promises a large prize.

### 5.7 Reciprocity

People may feel obligated to return a favour.

**Example:** An attacker offers a fake benefit and then requests sensitive information.

## 6. Social Engineering Attack Scenarios

### Scenario 1 — Fake IT Support

An employee receives a phone call from someone claiming to be from the organization's IT department.

The caller says there is an account problem and requests the employee's password.

**Security lesson:** Legitimate IT staff should use approved authentication and support procedures rather than asking users to disclose passwords.

### Scenario 2 — Malicious USB Drive

An unknown USB drive is found outside an office.

An employee connects it to a workstation out of curiosity.

**Security lesson:** Never connect unknown storage devices to personal or organizational systems.

### Scenario 3 — Fake Delivery Message

A victim receives a text message claiming that a package cannot be delivered until a small fee is paid.

The message contains a suspicious payment link.

**Security lesson:** Verify delivery information through the official delivery service.

## 7. Social Engineering Prevention

### Individual Security Practices

1. Verify identities before sharing information.
    
2. Never share passwords or OTPs.
    
3. Avoid clicking suspicious links.
    
4. Do not connect unknown USB devices.
    
5. Avoid disclosing confidential information publicly.
    
6. Use multi-factor authentication.
    
7. Verify unusual financial requests through a separate trusted channel.
    
8. Be cautious of urgent or threatening messages.
    

### Organizational Security Practices

1. Conduct regular security awareness training.
    
2. Establish identity verification procedures.
    
3. Implement physical access controls.
    
4. Apply least-privilege access.
    
5. Use email filtering and endpoint protection.
    
6. Establish clear incident-reporting procedures.
    
7. Securely dispose of confidential documents.
    
8. Perform authorized security assessments.
    

## 8. Social Engineering and Cybersecurity

Social engineering is important because technical security controls cannot prevent every form of human manipulation.

For example:

- A firewall cannot stop an employee from voluntarily sharing a password.
    
- Antivirus software cannot reliably prevent someone from transferring money to a fraudulent account.
    
- Strong encryption cannot prevent an authorized user from disclosing confidential information.
    

Effective cybersecurity requires both technical controls and human awareness.

## 9. Practical Lab — Social Engineering Awareness

This exercise uses fictional scenarios. Do not contact real people or attempt to obtain credentials.

### Task 1 — Identify the Technique

Identify the social engineering technique in each scenario.

**Scenario A**

An attacker calls an employee pretending to be a system administrator and requests their password.

**Scenario B**

Someone leaves a USB drive labelled "Exam Results" in a college computer lab.

**Scenario C**

A person follows an employee through a restricted office entrance.

**Scenario D**

A fake bank representative calls and asks for an OTP.

**Scenario E**

### Task 2 — Identify Psychological Triggers

For each scenario, identify the psychological trigger:

- Fear
    
- Urgency
    
- Authority
    
- Curiosity
    
- Greed
    
- Trust
    

### Task 3 — Write Defensive Responses

Write one safe response for each scenario.

Example:

**Situation:** Someone calls claiming to be IT support and requests your password.

**Safe response:** I will not share my password. I will contact the official IT department through a verified channel.

## 10. Social Engineering vs Technical Attacks

|Social Engineering|Technical Attack|
|---|---|
|Targets human behaviour|Targets systems or technology|
|Uses manipulation and deception|Uses technical weaknesses or malicious techniques|
|May involve impersonation|May involve exploitation of vulnerabilities|
|Example: Phishing|Example: SQL Injection|

Some cyberattacks combine both approaches.

For example, an attacker may use phishing to obtain credentials and then exploit an organization's technical systems.

## 11. Key Takeaways

- Social engineering targets human behaviour rather than only technical systems.
    
- Attackers exploit trust, fear, urgency, curiosity, and authority.
    
- Phishing, pretexting, baiting, and tailgating are common techniques.
    
- Social engineering can happen online, over the phone, or physically.
    
- Identity verification is an important defence.
    
- Security awareness training reduces human-related security risks.
    
- Technical controls and human awareness must work together.
